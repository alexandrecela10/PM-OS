00In one paragraph

Giant construction projects almost always finish late and over budget — and have done so at roughly the same rate for 70 years.

The reason isn't a shortage of software; it's that the information needed to run a project well is scattered across dozens of separate systems, dozens of separate companies, and millions of documents nobody can read fast enough. That's exactly the problem AI agents (software that can read across systems, reason over documents, and take actions with a human's approval) can now attack. This report maps the pain, the money, the competitors, where agents fit, and how to sell into the Gulf oil companies — backed by real quotes from the people who live it.

01Lifecycle

The end-to-end capital project lifecycle

Every mega-project moves through the same funnel of stages, each ending in a gate (a formal go/no-go decision you must pass to get funding for the next stage). The industry calls this stage-gating or FEL (Front-End Loading — doing the heavy planning up front, before committing the big money).

Two frameworks govern it: AACE estimate classes (AACE = Association for the Advancement of Cost Engineering; its "classes" rate how accurate a cost estimate is, 1–5) and CII/IPA stage-gate models (CII = Construction Industry Institute; IPA = Independent Project Analysis — research bodies that define best practice).

FEL-1 — Appraise / Concept Screening

  • What: Framing the business case; "is this idea worth studying?"
  • Who?The owner's business-development / strategy lead (owns the idea), the corporate finance / investment planner (owns the economics), and a subsurface or market specialist (owns the "is there really oil/demand here?" question).
  • Why?To kill bad ideas cheaply, before anyone spends real money. A wrong "yes" here isn't expensive yet — a wrong "yes" that survives to FID is catastrophic.
  • Estimate: AACE Class 5 — the roughest, accuracy about −50% to +100% (the real cost could be half, or double).
  • Gate: Worth spending money to study further?

FEL-2 — Select / Feasibility ("pre-FEED")

  • What: Comparing options and picking one concept.
  • Who?An emerging owner project manager (starts to own the project), a process / lead engineer (owns the technical concept), a cost estimator (prices each option), and pre-FEED consultants (do the studies).
  • Why?To lock in the most irreversible choice — which concept to build — while options are still cheap to change. IPA shows changing the concept after this point is one of the strongest predictors of an overrun; choosing wrong here bakes in cost no execution can undo.
  • Estimate: AACE Class 4 — accuracy about −30% to +50%.
  • Gate: Concept chosen; approval to start FEED.

FEL-3 — Define / FEED

FEED = Front-End Engineering Design (the detailed design stage that produces the blueprint and the budget the board votes on).

  • What: The make-or-break stage. Producing P&IDs (Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams — schematics of what connects to what), equipment lists, the build strategy, and the control estimate (the budget everything is later measured against).
  • Who?The owner's project director (accountable for the budget the board votes on), the owner's lead estimator (owns the control estimate), the FEED contractor's engineering manager (produces the design), and the owner's contracts lead (sets the build strategy).
  • Why?To turn a chosen concept into a defined, priceable project before the board commits billions — so the FID vote rests on a real number, not a hope. This is where cost, schedule and risk are actually determined; done poorly, every downstream overrun traces back to here.
  • Estimate: AACE Class 3 — accuracy about −20% to +30%.
  • Scope score: PDRI (Project Definition Rating Index — a checklist scoring how completely a project is defined; lower is better). Under 200 beats over 200 on cost, schedule and changes.
  • Gate: FID — Final Investment Decision (the moment the board approves the full budget and commits the billions). The budget promised here is what every "over-budget" number is later measured against.

EPC contracting / Award

EPC = Engineering, Procurement, Construction (one firm takes responsibility for designing, buying and building it).

  • Contract types: LSTK (Lump-Sum Turnkey — one fixed price, hands over a working plant; common in the Middle East) or EPCM / reimbursable (paid costs plus a fee; used when scope is riskier).
  • Who?The owner's procurement / contracts manager (runs the tender, picks the contract type), owner legal (owns risk allocation), and the bidding contractors' commercial teams (price and shape bids).
  • Why?To decide who builds it, on what terms, and — crucially — who carries the risk. The contract type sets the incentives for the whole multi-year build: a lump-sum deal pushes risk onto the contractor and quietly programs in the future claims war (see pain #3).

Detailed Engineering, Procurement, Construction (Execution)

  • What: Full design, long-lead procurement (ordering equipment that takes many months to build, like giant compressors), expediting (chasing suppliers to deliver on time), and construction.
  • Who?Contractor side: the EPC project manager (owns delivery + profit), planners, quantity surveyors (commercial/claims), procurement / expediting. Owner side: the PMT (Project Management Team — owner oversight staff) — its project controls and contracts team.
  • Why?To build the thing while keeping it on the FID budget and schedule. This is where money is spent and overruns become visible, so the real job is early detection — catching a slip, a cost drift, or a claim while it's still cheap to fix. Most software targets here, yet the damage is usually baked in from FEED.
  • How long: 3–6+ years for a giga-project.

Commissioning, Start-up & Handover

  • What: Testing, switching on safely, and handing over the asset and its data. Tracked at the level of individual tags (unique ID codes for every valve, pump and instrument — a plant has tens of thousands).
  • Who?The commissioning manager (drives start-up), completions engineers (track readiness tag-by-tag), the handover / document-control team (assemble the data package), and the owner's O&M receiving team (Operations & Maintenance — runs the plant for decades) who must accept it.
  • Why?To prove the plant works and hand operations a complete, trustworthy record so they can run it safely for decades. This is when the investment starts earning — so every day of delay is lost revenue at the worst moment: capital fully spent, nothing yet produced.
  • Gates: Mechanical completion → RFSU (Ready For Start-Up) → acceptance.

Operations & Maintenance (O&M)

  • What: 20–40 years of running the asset — the reason it was all built.
  • Who?The operations team (runs the plant), maintenance / reliability engineers (keep it running), and later brownfield project teams (the people who design upgrades to an existing plant) who inherit the same data.
  • Why?To turn the built asset into decades of safe, efficient production. Good handover data makes maintenance cheap and the next expansion easy; bad data means engineers spend years re-discovering how their own plant is built.
The key takeaway

The budget is decided at the very start (FEED/FID) but the overrun only shows up years later during construction. Most software only helps during construction — by then the damage is already baked in.

02Org chart

Who does what

Owner side (Aramco/ADNOC's own project staff)

  • Capital Projects / Project Director — owns the whole budget and schedule. Bad week: the board asks why the forecast jumped $2bn. Judged on: delivering to the sanctioned (board-approved) cost/schedule, safety, first-oil date.
  • PMO (Project Management Office — the central reporting team) / Project Controls — maintain the schedule, cost report and EAC (Estimate At Completion — the live forecast of the final cost). Bad week: finding the contractor's reported progress is inflated.
  • Cost Engineers — track commitments vs actuals (money promised vs money spent).
  • Contracts & Procurement — defend against claims (formal contractor requests for more money/time). Bad week: a flood of claims arriving faster than they can be assessed.
  • Interface Managers — manage boundaries between contractors (where one firm's work must connect to another's). Bad week: both assumed the other was building the connection.
  • Commissioning Managers — drive start-up. Bad week: thousands of punch items (small unfinished/defective jobs on a snagging list) block switch-on.

Contractor side (Bechtel, Fluor, Saipem…)

  • EPC Project Manager — owns delivery and margin (profit).
  • Planners — build the schedule in Primavera P6 (the industry-standard scheduling software, by Oracle); watch the critical path (the task sequence that sets the finish date).
  • Quantity Surveyors (QS) (measure work done and price changes — standard in Commonwealth/Middle East contracts).
  • Document Controllers — manage transmittals (formal document hand-offs) in Aconex (Oracle's document platform, the standard on big projects).

03Tools

The tool landscape

  • Scheduling: Primavera P6 (~$3,520/user to buy, ~$1,320/user/yr cloud).
  • Cost / forecasting: Hexagon EcoSys (a project-controls platform; from ~$40k), SAP (the big finance/ERP system holding the owner's commitments and actuals), and Excel everywhere.
  • Documents: Aconex, SharePoint, OpenText.
  • Engineering: Hexagon, AVEVA, Bentley (3D design + engineering data).
  • Construction: Procore, Autodesk Construction Cloud, InEight.
  • Completions: WinPCS, Smart Completions, Coreworx.
  • Progress capture: OpenSpace, Buildots, Doxel, Disperse (cameras/lasers that photograph a site and compare it to the plan).
Why the data is stuck

Each party keeps its own system with its own coding structure (private numbering — a WBS, the hierarchical to-do list, and a CBS, the matching cost buckets). Owner SAP doesn't talk to contractor cost systems; the schedule is disconnected from what's ordered and designed; correspondence sits as unstructured PDFs. KPMG (a Big Four consultancy): a third of owners haven't connected their project system to accounting. NIST (US standards institute): ~$15.8bn/yr lost just to systems not exchanging data. Reporting is monthly batch — weeks old before anyone sees a problem.

Emerging AI players (2026)

  • ALICE Technologies — AI schedule optimisation; McKinsey alliance (14 Apr 2026); up to 20% schedule cuts; launched a "chat with your schedule" agent.
  • nPlan — AI schedule-risk forecasting on 750,000+ past schedules; $16M Series B (Oct 2025) with Chevron's venture arm; used on HS2, Network Rail, Anglian Water.
  • Document Crunch — AI contract review; bought by Trimble for $246.4M (Apr 2026); 400+ customers incl. Balfour Beatty, DPR.
  • Procore — bought Datagrid (Jan 2026); launched a suite of AI agents (May 2026) across specs/drawings/RFIs (Requests For Information — formal questions from builder to designer).
The pattern

2026 is a consolidation year — incumbents are buying AI startups. Almost all of it is contractor-side and construction-phase. The owner-side, cross-system, front-end and claims layers stay open — that's the gap.

04Pain

Pain points ranked by severity

The headline numbers

McKinsey: ~79% average overrun (532 projects); 98% of megaprojects overrun >30%. Flyvbjerg (the Oxford professor whose 16,000-project database is definitive): only 0.5% finish on budget, on time and on benefits — the "Iron Law." EY: in 365 oil-and-gas megaprojects, 64% over budget, 73% late, 59% average cost growth, $500bn total — and the Middle East is the worst region on Earth (89% over cost).

  • #1 Cost overruns at/after FID (master pain). Root cause: optimism bias & strategic misrepresentation (estimates are systematically too low — partly over-optimism, partly because low numbers win approval). Cases: Kashagan (~$50bn, ~11 yrs late), Gorgon LNG ($37→54bn), Ichthys ($34→45bn), Channel Tunnel (80% over).
  • #2 Schedule slippage. HKA (a claims/disputes consultancy) found contractors sought time extensions averaging 66% of the plan. Old-school QSRA (the standard statistical schedule-risk method) can't beat the bias.
  • #3 Claims & disputes. HKA: $95bn claimed, disputes averaging 33% of budget. Arcadis (publishes an annual disputes report): average US dispute $60.1M, ~12.5 months. Entitlement (the legal right to more money/time) is matched to the contract by hand, and deadlines get missed.
  • #4 Interface failures. INCOSE study: 4% cost growth with mature interface management vs 18% without.
  • #5 Handover chaos. CFIHOS (the industry standard for what handover data must be delivered) / ISO 19650 (the international standard for managing asset information): a "six-month bottleneck" of manual cross-referencing.
  • #6 EAC reconciliation. Only ~25–31% of projects finish within 10% of budget; the monthly forecast is reconciled by hand.

05Structure

Why the industry is still broken

  1. Fragmented data with bespoke coding — no shared source of truth.
  2. Adversarial fixed-price contracts — the contract that shifts risk also rewards claiming and hiding bad news. Only ~32% of owners highly trust their contractors.
  3. Optimism bias & strategic misrepresentation — low estimates win approval.
  4. Every project is a one-off — teams disband; lessons evaporate.
  5. Thin contractor margins — Fluor ~0.13% net (2023); no cushion, so contractors must claim to survive.
  6. Monthly batch reporting — problems are weeks old before anyone sees them.
  7. Signal buried in unstructured text — emails and letters, unreadable at scale until now.

The through-line: the information that could save a project already exists — it's just scattered and unreadable fast enough. That is exactly what AI agents are built to do.

06Opportunity

Where agentic AI fits

  • A. Claims-defence & entitlement-recovery agent — highest owner value. In construction, contractors claim up to the owner; the owner's job is to defend and to recover its own entitlements. So for an owner this agent (1) helps rebut inflated contractor claims — IPA (Independent Project Analysis — the definitive capital-projects benchmarking body) found contractors claim about twice what they settle for; (2) catches where a contractor missed its own notice (the deadline-bound letter that preserves a right — miss it and the claim can be lost) deadline; and (3) pursues the owner's own liquidated damages (pre-agreed penalties for the contractor finishing late) and back-charges (costs recovered from the contractor for defective work) — off a record the owner controls. Wow: "This $40M contractor claim rests on 6 events — for 3 the contractor missed its own deadline, and it's priced ~2× comparable settlements; here's the rebuttal, cited." Possible now because LLMs (Large Language Models — AI that reads and reasons over ordinary documents) can read thousands of documents against legal language. Note: the loudest quotable pain here is the contractor's (under-claiming); the owner's is defence + recovery — pitch it that way, not "you forgot to claim."
  • B. FID red-team agent — highest strategic leverage. Before the board commits, stress-tests the estimate against a reference class (comparable past projects and how they actually turned out — Flyvbjerg's proven anti-bias method) and audits the FEED for gaps. Wow: "This resembles projects that overran 60%; here are its 9 weakest scope elements."
  • C. Cost reconciliation (EAC) agent. Matches owner SAP to contractor cost data despite mismatched coding. Wow: "Your forecast is $180M light — here are the 6 drivers."
  • D. Schedule-truth agent. Cross-examines each P6 update against what's actually ordered and designed.
  • E. Handover completeness agent. Validates handover data against CFIHOS/ISO 19650; flags missing certificates.
  • F. Interface agent. Spots gaps/overlaps between packages before assembly.
  • G. Design-feasibility gate. On every design revision during FEED, runs three checks in minutes and returns a cited pass/fail list: compliant? (vs the Saudi/Dubai building code), within cost? (vs the budget ceiling), buildable? (constructability + long-lead clashes). A "spell-check for feasibility" that catches design errors while they cost thousands, not the tens of millions they become in the field. Possible now because vision/language models can read thousands of pages of drawings and code and reason about conflicts.
Strategic read — owner-first prioritisation

Scored from the owner's seat on three axes — value to the owner, delivery confidence today, and how little effort/data the owner must give up. Lead with A (claims-defence) and G (design-feasibility gate): both are high-value, buildable now, and run largely on data the owner already holds (contracts & correspondence for A; design files for G), so they cost the owner least to adopt. B (FID red-team) is the highest-value prize but a harder first sale — it needs the owner's own historical data and lands an unwelcome message at the highest-stakes gate, so it's the ideal second move. C (cost), D (schedule-truth), E (handover) and F (interface) rank lower: deeper system integration or data the owner can't hand over cheaply. Net: lead with A and G, follow with B.

Validating Opportunity G — the design-feasibility gate

The pain and value here are unusually well-quantified, and the strongest signal is that a government is building this itself.

A fully integrated digital system capable of issuing building permits automatically and without human intervention by reading submitted drawings and documents, verifying compliance with the Dubai Building Code.

— Dubai Municipality, on its AI permit system (26-week rollout), 2026 · zawya.com

Why it matters: when the regulator automates the compliance check, every developer and consultant must arrive pre-compliant — that is the demand for the private, owner-side version. It also confirms the three-discipline scope (plot, architecture, MEP, structural) is technically doable now.
We reduced our SBC compliance review cycle from 6 weeks to 3 days. The AI caught two fire environmental gaps that would have delayed our building permit by months.

— Customer testimonial, WhiteHelmet (Saudi-based construction intelligence), 2026 · whitehelmet.sa

Why it matters: a Gulf customer quantifying both time (~90% faster) and avoided delay (permit delay of "months" prevented) on the exact Saudi Building Code this product targets.
What used to take us weeks is now done in days… Every coordination issue that Buildcheck caught saved us from expensive field modifications and potential delays.

Why it matters: confirms the "catch it in design, not in the field" value from a paying user's mouth.
Quantified value (all sourced)

Rework ≈ 5% of project cost on average (CII IR-153; 90th-pct 12.4%), design rework +1–3 pts. ~70–80% of cost deviations trace to design, not execution. An error caught in design costs ~10× less than in construction, ~100× less than after handover (CII 1-10-100); pre-construction review catches 60%+ of rework-causing errors. Independent ROI: 4:1 to 30:1 cost-avoidance; an RFI is $200–$500 at drawing stage vs $2,000–$8,000 in the field. Documented outcomes: design errors reaching the field cut 50–75%, schedule compressed 8–18%. On a $5bn giga-project, ~5% rework = ~$250M, of which ~28% (~$70M) is design-caused; catching half early avoids ~$35M on a single project — before schedule/first-oil value. On a $20bn project the design-caused figure is ~$280M. (Sources: reworkcost.com/CII IR-153, mycomply.net, helonic.com, miragemetrics.com, tommasomariaricci.com)

Honest gap

Strong practitioner and vendor validation (WhiteHelmet, Buildcheck) and a decisive regulator signal (Dubai Municipality), but no on-record quote from an Aramco/ADNOC owner executive naming this specific tool — treat owner-persona demand as inferred from the regulator move and the mandatory SBC 2024 reset, and confirm in primary interviews.

08Buying

Buying reality: how Gulf owners buy

Saudi Aramco

  • SAP Ariba (Aramco's mandatory online supplier portal) registration — needs an in-Kingdom entity via MISA (the ministry that licenses foreign companies). ~3–6 months.
  • IKTVA (In-Kingdom Total Value Add — Aramco's local-content score; a weak score hurts every bid).
  • SACS-002 → CCC (a mandatory cybersecurity standard; you must earn a Compliance Certificate before touching Aramco data).
  • PDPL (Saudi data-protection law) / data residency — sensitive data hosted in-Kingdom by default.
  • Front doors: Wa'ed Ventures ($500M VC arm), SAIL (innovation lab), Aramco Digital.

ADNOC (UAE)

  • ICV (In-Country Value — the UAE equivalent of IKTVA).
  • Sovereign cloud — G42's Khazna plus Microsoft/AWS/Google/Oracle UAE regions.
  • AIQ (the ADNOC–G42 AI joint venture) won the $340M deal. ADNOC prefers to build agentic AI through AIQ — so partner with them, don't compete.

Deal shape

  • Pricing: % of project value (Procore charges on ACV (Annual Construction Volume), ~0.1–0.3% of hard costs). On a $10bn project, 0.05% = $5M — trivial vs one $23M claim.
  • Path: paid PoC (Proof of Concept — a small paid pilot on 1–2 live projects) → multi-year contract.
  • Cycle: 9–18+ months first contact to contract.

09Plan

Recommendations

  1. Pick the wedge now: lead with the two lowest-effort, highest-confidence owner wedges — claims-defence & entitlement-recovery (A) and the design-feasibility gate (G), both running mostly on data the owner already holds. Bring in the FID red-team (B) as the high-value second move once inside and trusted. Add cost (C) and schedule-truth (D) later.
  2. Make compliance a feature: in-Kingdom/in-UAE hosting + SACS-002/CCC + IKTVA/ICV from day one.
  3. Enter through the innovation arms: Wa'ed / SAIL / Aramco Digital; partner with AIQ / Hub71. Run a paid PoC with a pre-agreed success metric.
  4. Price to value: a fraction of project value; one avoided claim pays for it many times over.

10Caveats

Caveats & evidence gaps

  • Kashagan's larger lifetime figures are secondary financial press — treat as estimates.
  • Interface-management quantification rests largely on one INCOSE dataset.
  • No single clean "cost of poor handover" percentage exists; the six-month bottleneck and NIST $15.8bn/yr are the anchors.
  • Gulf sign-off authority and cycle length are thin in public sources — validate with interviews.
  • No verified deployment of nPlan/ALICE specifically at Aramco or ADNOC was found.
  • Several vendor metrics (nPlan's $1.2bn saved; ALICE's savings) are self-reported.
