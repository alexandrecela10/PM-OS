The one rule behind every screen

Nothing here asks anyone to change how they already work. The agent reads files the owner already has, it shows up where the person already looks, and it never sends anything itself.

  • Read-only inputs, no migration, no new system of record
  • Arrives in email or Teams, reviewed on one screen
  • Every line clicks through to a page in a real document
  • Ranked by money, so 20 minutes covers the day
  • Proven on closed projects before it touches a live one

Product 1 · claims defence & recovery · Faisal Al-Harbi

The chosen journey: an always-on agent, and a 20-minute morning review

Persona: Faisal Al-Harbi, the owner's contracts and commercial manager on Jebel Nasr Train 4. His job is to rebut inflated claims, catch where the contractor missed its own deadlines, and collect the owner's own late-delivery damages and back-charges.

0 · CONNECT

Read-only copy of the contract and the letters. One afternoon, IT only.

1 · WATCH

Agent reads new documents overnight, every night. No screen involved.

2 · DIGEST

Morning email: three things worth money today.

3 · QUEUE

One screen, ranked by money, with deadlines counting down.

4 · OPEN

The evidence chain, and how the money was estimated.

5 · DECIDE

Approve, edit or reject. The human is the gate.

6 · DRAFT

The letter or rebuttal pack, cited, ready to send.

7 · TRACK

What was sent, what came back, what it settled at.

8 · REPORT

Month-end brief written on demand, then thrown away.

Why always on: the events that create or destroy entitlement happen daily, and the deadlines that kill them are counted in days. A monthly product cannot save a 28-day notice window. This is the one place where "always on" is the value, not a feature.

Why human in the loop on the money: the agent's job is to find and size the move. Deciding to spend a commercial position is the manager's job, and it stays that way. That's also what makes the first sale possible.

Deliberately no dashboard

There's no live wall of charts here, and that's a decision. A dashboard makes a person go and look. An always-on agent comes to Faisal, and only when there's money on the table.

What "always on" means concretely

Documents are read as they land, not at month-end. Deadlines are recalculated daily. So the product's promise is a countdown, not a report: you will never learn about a notice window after it closed.

If nothing worth money happened, the agent says nothing. Silence is a feature. The fastest way to lose a commercial manager is to send them a daily email they learn to ignore.

Why email is the product surface

The adoption cost of a new tool is mostly the cost of remembering it exists. This person already lives in mail. So the digest is the product, and the web screen is where they go once they've decided something is worth ten minutes.

Counter-metric we watch: how often the digest gets ignored two days running. That, not usage, tells us if the ranking is honest.

Product 2 · design feasibility · Priya Raghavan

The chosen journey: a check that runs on the revision you were issuing anyway

Persona: Priya Raghavan, the owner's design and engineering manager on Jebel Nasr Train 4. Her job is to issue drawings that are legal, affordable and buildable, before mistakes harden into rework in the field.

0 · SET RULES

Which code edition, which budget ceilings. Once, at project start.

1 · DROP

A revision lands in the folder they already use. No new step.

2 · CHECK

Legal, affordable, buildable. Minutes, not weeks.

3 · READ

A cited pass and fail list, worst first, with the money attached.

4 · ARGUE

Open a finding, see the clause and the drawing region, accept or dispute.

5 · ISSUE

Issue the revision with a clean sheet, or issue with known exceptions on the record.

This is the lowest-effort product of the three, and that's why it ships alongside Product 1. It attaches to an action the designer already performs, so adoption doesn't need a behaviour change, a login habit or a champion.

The demand signal isn't a survey, it's a regulator: Dubai Municipality is building automatic permit issue by reading submitted drawings against the building code. When the check becomes automatic on the outside, arriving pre-checked stops being optional.

Advisory, never blocking

The check can't stop a revision being issued. An engineer who's been overruled by a machine once will find a way around it forever, so the product argues its case and then gets out of the way.

Disputes are the training data

Every dispute reason is a labelled error. Wrong code edition and misread drawing are engineering bugs. Not applicable and already waived are missing context we should have had. Both get fixed, and we report the rate.

The sacrifice here is professional, not commercial: a machine is marking an engineer's work inside the engineer's own workflow. Quoting the clause verbatim and making disagreement one click is how you make that bearable.

The engineering flow

One reading layer, nine steps, three products on top

 FILES THE OWNER ALREADY HAS
 contract · letters & minutes · schedule updates · site reports · drawings · code editions · cost ceilings
        |
   1 READ AND FILE        every page split, dated, attributed to a party, tagged to a drawing or an activity
        |
   2 BUILD THE RECORD     one linked timeline: events, obligations, revisions, deadlines, quantities
        |
   3 APPLY THE RULES      deadline maths · damages · code clauses · cost bounds        [ code, not the model ]
        |
   4 REASON OVER IT       "does this event trigger this clause?"                       [ the model, with citations ]
                             "does this revision close last round's comments?"
        |
   5 SIZE THE MONEY       value at stake · confidence · what happens if you do nothing
        |
   6 SHOW YOUR WORKING    no citation, no finding. Uncited claims are dropped, not softened
        |
   7 A HUMAN DECIDES      approve · edit · reject, on a queue ranked by money
        |
   8 DRAFT THE ACTION     the letter · the rebuttal pack · the verdict list · the challenge book
        |
   9 LEARN FROM OUTCOMES  what was approved, what was rejected, what it settled at        -> back into 4 and 5

The split at steps 3 and 4 is the whole engineering thesis. Anything that ends up as a number in a letter is computed in code, so it's reproducible and arguable. The model reads, links and proposes.

Products 2 and 3 reuse steps 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 9 unchanged. That's why the second product costs a fraction of the first, and why the roadmap items later are weeks of work, not years.

Three failures we design against by name: inventing a document, citing a real document that says something else, and being confidently wrong about a date. The first two are caught at step 6, the third can't happen because step 3 owns the dates.

Cost control: cheap models read and tag at volume, the expensive reasoning runs only on candidate events. Cost per document read is a tracked metric, because a product that reads everything daily has to be cheap to run.

"It found what our commercial team found, plus one thing they missed, all cited" is the pilot's success test. It's also the eval, which is the point: the demo and the test are the same artefact.

Learning, but governed

Rejections and disputes are labelled data. They tune ranking, thresholds and clause mapping. They never silently change what the rules layer computes, and no customer's material trains anything shared.

Deployment constraints, treated as features

In-region private cloud from day one. No training on customer data. A read audit trail showing every document the agent opened and why. Cyber certification and local-content requirements sit on the roadmap, not in a compliance annex at the end.

The counter-metric

The adoption promise

Six rules that make this the cheapest thing a project team ever adopted

1 · Their own files, read-only

No migration, no new system of record, no agreement about coding structures. If it needs a data project first, we've already lost.

2 · No new habit

Product 1 arrives in the inbox. Product 2 hooks the file drop. Product 3 shows up as the sponsor's own pre-read.

3 · The agent never sends

It drafts. A person presses send. Every time, in every product.

4 · Every line is clickable

No assertion without a page in a real document behind it. Uncited findings are dropped, not softened.

5 · Proof on history first

Run on closed projects, show what it would have caught, then go near a live one.

6 · Priced against one avoided loss

Pilot small enough to sit inside one project team's own authority. The market's deal shape is a paid pilot, then a large multi-year contract.

Each rule is a design constraint on every screen in this prototype, not a promise in a sales deck. If a screen breaks one of these rules, the screen is wrong.
